\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A biochemical process is conventionally specified by naming its participants:
which species, which complexes, which cofactors, in which order. We show that
this specification contains a part that does no work. A process is fully
determined by its \emph{residue chain}---a finite sequence of steps in which the
residue deposited by one step is the boundary condition of the next---together
with, for each step, a single number in $[0,1]$ recording the fraction of the
remaining gap to the target that the step closes. Nothing else about a step
enters. We call such a step a \emph{rung}, such a sequence a \emph{ladder}, and
we prove that the identities are inert: two processes with the same power
sequence are the same ladder, and no observable of the process distinguishes
them. The account rests on two primitives, both derived here rather than
assumed. First, individuation costs an irreducible floor $\flo > 0$, because
drawing a boundary takes time during which the complement moves. Second, and
consequently, there is an asymmetry between \emph{probing} a region---entering
it along a trajectory, which draws no boundary and is free---and
\emph{committing} it---individuating it, which costs at least $\flo$ regardless
of how improbable the region is. From these we obtain: composition is
multiplicative, $\pow(\Rung_1\cdots\Rung_n) = 1 - \prod_i(1-\pow_i)$; repetition
of a single rung has strictly diminishing returns and never reaches the target;
a ladder closes its gap in the limit if and only if $\sum_i \pow_i$ diverges;
and a structure that has been committed once is thereafter traversed by probes
alone, which is catalysis without consumption, obtained from the asymmetry and
not from an energetic argument. We give a small-step operational semantics with
progress, preservation and monotone commitment, and a static analysis that
rejects at compile time a ladder whose declared target its declared rungs cannot
reach. The framework is demonstrated on a chain of length four presented purely
as a power sequence: we show that the conventional description of that chain is
recoverable from no observable the formalism admits, and that every question
usually asked of it---where the control lies, what a modification is worth,
whether two chains are the same process---is answered from the powers alone.
Classification and improvement of catalysts thereby become operations on
numbers rather than on names.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{What a process description contains}

Ask for a description of a biochemical process and you receive a list of
participants in an order: these species, transformed by these agents, through
these intermediates. The description is detailed, it is checkable against
experiment, and it is how the process is taught. It is also, we shall argue,
partly inert---it contains material that no observable of the process depends
on, and which can be deleted without loss.

The claim is not that the identities are unknown, or unimportant for other
purposes, or that a chemist should stop naming things. It is narrower and
sharper: \emph{given a process, the identities of its participants do not enter
any question the process itself answers}. What enters is the sequence of
changes and, for each change, how much of the remaining distance to the outcome
it accomplishes. Two processes agreeing on that sequence agree on everything,
however different their participants.

This is a strong claim and we prove it, in the precise form of
\cref{thm:inertness}: a process is a residue chain with a power sequence, and no
observable admitted by the formalism separates two processes with the same power
sequence.

\subsection{Why one would want this}

Three practical questions become tractable when the identities are removed.

\paragraph{Classification.} Asking whether two catalysts are ``the same kind''
currently means comparing mechanisms, folds, active-site residues, or
sequence homology, and these criteria disagree with one another
\citep{khersonsky2010}. On the present account two catalysts are the same rung
if they have the same power, and there is nothing further to compare. The
criterion is a single number and it is measurable.

\paragraph{Improvement.} Asking how to improve a catalyst currently means
proposing a modification and testing it. On the present account improvement is
an increase in a rung's power, and two results say where the headroom is.
\Cref{cor:diversify} rules out repetition: copies of what already works have
geometrically diminishing returns. \Cref{prop:marginal} then identifies the rung
whose improvement buys the most, and its answer is not the one intuition
supplies---see \cref{rem:direction}.

\paragraph{Comparison across scales.} A ladder is a ladder whether its rungs are
molecular, cellular, or industrial. The composition law
(\cref{thm:multiplicative}) does not know what a rung is made of, so processes
that share no chemistry can be compared directly.

\subsection{What has to be derived}

We take nothing from a prior formalism. Two things must be established before
the account can be stated:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.4em]
\item \textbf{The floor.} That individuating anything costs an irreducible
positive amount. This is \cref{sec:floor}, and it is derived from the
observation that drawing a boundary takes time during which what lies outside it
changes.
\item \textbf{The asymmetry.} That entering a region and individuating a region
are operations of different kinds, the first free and the second floored. This
is \cref{sec:asymmetry}. It is what makes reuse possible, and hence what makes
catalysis possible.
\end{enumerate}

Everything else---rungs, ladders, composition, the semantics, the static
analysis---follows.

\subsection{Organisation}

\Cref{part:substrate} derives the floor and the asymmetry.
\Cref{part:ladders} defines rungs and ladders and proves the composition
results. \Cref{part:inertness} contains the central theorem: the identities do
not enter. \Cref{part:machine} gives the operational semantics, the static
analysis, and the cost model. \Cref{part:demo} works a chain of length four
entirely in powers. \Cref{part:validation} states the predictions and the
computational protocol.

\section{The Floor}
\label{sec:floor}

\subsection{Individuation takes time}

\begin{postulate}[Existence by distinction]
\label{post:negation}
An item exists as a distinct entity if and only if there is a well-defined
complement from which it is distinguished. Its existence consists in the
distinction and not in any property it possesses independently of it.
\end{postulate}

\begin{postulate}[Distinction takes time]
\label{post:duration}
Establishing a distinction requires a finite non-zero interval $\tau > 0$,
during which the complement evolves.
\end{postulate}

\begin{remark}
\Cref{post:duration} is forced, not chosen. Were $\tau = 0$, the state of the
world immediately before the distinction and immediately after would coincide;
but a distinction that changes nothing has not been drawn. Hence $\tau > 0$, and
the second clause follows for any world that is not static.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Boundary thickness]
\label{def:thickness}
Let $A$ be individuated over $[t_0, t_0+\tau]$. Its \emph{boundary thickness} is
the measure of the set of states which, at completion, belong determinately
neither to $A$ nor to its complement.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Thickness is positive]
\label{thm:thickpos}
Any individuation carried out under
\cref{post:negation,post:duration} has strictly positive boundary thickness.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{post:duration} the operation occupies $\tau > 0$ and the complement
evolves during it. Consider a state lying outside $A$ at $t_0$ and inside the
region assigned to $A$ at $t_0+\tau$, or conversely; such states exist whenever
the complement is non-static, which \cref{post:duration} guarantees. For such a
state the assignment appropriate at the start and the assignment appropriate at
completion disagree, so it is determinately in neither part. The set of such
states is non-empty and its measure is the thickness.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The floor is uniform when refinement is capped}

\Cref{thm:thickpos} gives positivity separately for each individuation. The
results below need a bound holding uniformly, and positivity of each member of a
family does not give positivity of the infimum: the numbers $1/n$ are all
positive with infimum zero. A hypothesis is therefore required, and we state it
rather than let it pass unnoticed.

\begin{definition}[Contact graph]
\label{def:graph}
A \emph{contact graph} is $\Gph = (V, E, \wt)$ with $V$ finite and non-empty,
$E$ a set of unordered pairs of distinct vertices, and $\wt : E \to \Rpos$
\citep{diestel2010}. A distinguished vertex $\med \in V$, the \emph{medium}, is
adjacent to every other vertex; the remainder are \emph{items}. The weight of a
contact is the cost of maintaining the distinction between its endpoints.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Cut, residue, floor]
\label{def:cut}
For $S \subseteq V$ containing at least one item and omitting $\med$, the
\emph{cut} is $\cut(S) = \{\{u,v\} \in E : u \in S,\, v \notin S\}$ and the
\emph{residue} is $\res(S) = \sum_{e \in \cut(S)} \wt(e)$. The \emph{floor} is
$\flo = \min_{e \in E} \wt(e)$ and the \emph{total} is
$\Tot = \sum_{e \in E} \wt(e)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Positive floor, positive residue]
\label{lem:floor}
$\flo > 0$, and every admissible cut satisfies $\res(S) \geq \flo > 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$E$ is finite and non-empty and every weight is strictly positive, so $\flo$ is
the minimum of a finite non-empty set of positive reals and is positive. Since
$S$ contains an item $v$ and omits $\med$, and $\{v,\med\} \in E$ because $\med$
is adjacent to every item, $\cut(S)$ is non-empty. Its weight is a sum of terms
each at least $\flo$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Where finiteness does the work]
\label{rem:finiteness}
\Cref{lem:floor} is a statement about a \emph{finite} graph, and the finiteness
is not decoration. A system able to refine without limit generates thicknesses
with infimum zero, and for such a system no uniform floor exists. Every result
downstream inherits this hypothesis. We verify both branches numerically in
\cref{sec:results}, because a hypothesis that is never tested against its own
failure case is not a hypothesis but a habit.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Alignment]
\label{def:alignment}
Fix a target region $\target$. The \emph{alignment} of a region $x$ to $\target$
is the minimum residue separating them,
\begin{equation}
\aln(x, \target) \;=\; \min_{S \ni x,\ \target \notin S} \res(S),
\label{eq:alignment}
\end{equation}
computable in polynomial time by maximum flow \citep{ford1956,stoer1997}. The
\emph{normalised alignment} is $a(x,\target) = \aln(x,\target)/\Tot$.
\end{definition}

Alignment measures distance-to-target as separation cost. A region far from the
target is expensive to separate from it; a region at the target is separated
only at the floor.

\begin{lemma}[Alignment is floor-bounded]
\label{lem:alignfloor}
$a(x,\target) \geq \flo/\Tot > 0$ for every $x$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from \cref{lem:floor} applied to the minimising cut.
\end{proof}

\section{The Asymmetry}
\label{sec:asymmetry}

We now derive the distinction on which everything else rests.

\begin{definition}[Probe and commit]
\label{def:probecommit}
Let $T$ be a measure-preserving traversal of the state space
\citep{walters1982}. A region $W$ is \emph{probed} when some iterate of $T$
enters it: this is a fact about a trajectory and involves no boundary. A region
$W$ is \emph{committed} when a boundary is drawn individuating $W$ from its
complement: this is an act, and by \cref{thm:thickpos} it deposits residue.
\end{definition}

The two relations are of different kinds. Probing relates a trajectory to a
point; committing relates an agent to a region. At every instant a trajectory
occupies exactly one position, probed because the traversal took it there, with
nothing committing anything.

\begin{theorem}[Probe--commit asymmetry]
\label{thm:asymmetry}
Let $W$ be a region of positive measure $p(W) > 0$ in a finite-measure space,
and $T$ a measure-preserving traversal. Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2.4em]
\item \textbf{Committing costs the floor.} Any commitment of $W$ deposits
residue at least $\flo > 0$, and this bound does not depend on $p(W)$.
\item \textbf{Probing is free.} Almost every trajectory enters $W$, and does so
without any boundary being drawn and without residue.
\item \textbf{Rarity affects frequency only.} The long-run frequency with which
$W$ is probed equals $p(W)$. A rare region is probed as freely as a common one,
merely less often.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) A commitment individuates $W$, so by \cref{thm:thickpos} its boundary has
positive thickness, and by \cref{lem:floor} the associated cut has residue at
least $\flo$. Neither step refers to $p(W)$.

(ii) By the Poincar\'{e} recurrence theorem \citep{poincare1890,walters1982},
for a measure-preserving $T$ on a finite-measure space and any $W$ with
$p(W) > 0$, almost every point of $W$ returns to $W$ infinitely often, and by
ergodicity of the induced map almost every point of the space enters $W$. Entry
is a property of the trajectory. \Cref{def:probecommit} attaches no boundary to
it, and by \cref{thm:thickpos} residue arises only from boundaries.

(iii) The Birkhoff ergodic theorem gives, for almost every initial point,
$\lim_{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k<n}\mathbf{1}_W(T^k x) = p(W)$. This is a statement
about frequency and carries no cost term.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Reuse is possible]
\label{cor:reuse}
A structure that has been committed once thereafter renders the region it
individuates available to probing, and every subsequent probe of that region is
free. Such a structure is not consumed by the probes that traverse it, and may
be traversed without limit.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The commitment is performed once and deposits residue once, by
\cref{thm:asymmetry}(i). Thereafter the region is individuated and a trajectory
entering it is probing, by \cref{def:probecommit}. By \cref{thm:asymmetry}(ii)
probing deposits nothing. Since nothing is deposited, nothing is spent, and the
number of traversals is unbounded.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[This is catalysis, and it is not an energetic argument]
\label{rem:catalysis}
\Cref{cor:reuse} is the formal content of the observation that a catalyst is not
consumed. It does not appeal to free energy, to a cycle that regenerates the
catalyst, or to a convention that catalysts are excluded from stoichiometry. The
catalyst is never spent because what passes through it probes rather than
commits, and probing is free by \cref{thm:asymmetry}(ii). The asymmetry is doing
all the work, and it was derived from the floor, which was derived from the fact
that individuation takes time.
\end{remark}

\section{Rungs}
\label{sec:rungs}

\begin{definition}[Rung and power]
\label{def:rung}
A \emph{rung} toward target $\target$ is a partial map $\Rung$ on regions whose
application commits a contact and yields $\Rung(x)$. Its \emph{power} at $x$ is
the fraction of the above-floor alignment that it closes,
\begin{equation}
\pow(\Rung, x) \;=\;
\frac{a(x,\target) - a(\Rung(x),\target)}{a(x,\target) - \flo/\Tot}
\;\in\; [0,1].
\label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
A rung is \emph{inert} if $\pow = 0$ and \emph{absolute} if $\pow = 1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Power is bounded]
\label{lem:powbound}
$\pow(\Rung,x) \in [0,1]$ for every rung, region and target.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:alignfloor}, $a(\Rung(x),\target) \geq \flo/\Tot$, so the numerator
of \cref{eq:power} never exceeds the denominator and $\pow \leq 1$. A map that
increases alignment is not a rung toward $\target$; by convention such a map is
assigned $\pow = 0$, giving $\pow \geq 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What a rung is not]
\label{rem:rungisnot}
\Cref{def:rung} mentions no property of $\Rung$ beyond the alignment it closes.
It does not say what $\Rung$ is made of, how it operates, how long it takes, or
what passes through it. Two maps with the same power at $x$ are, at this
resolution, the same rung. That this is not a loss of information is the content
of \cref{thm:inertness}.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Ladder]
\label{def:ladder}
A \emph{ladder} is a finite sequence of rungs $\Rung_1,\dots,\Rung_n$ applied in
order, each to the output of the last. Its \emph{power sequence} is
$(\pow_1,\dots,\pow_n)$ with $\pow_i = \pow(\Rung_i, x_{i-1})$ and
$x_i = \Rung_i(x_{i-1})$.
\end{definition}

\section{Composition}
\label{sec:composition}

\begin{theorem}[Multiplicative composition]
\label{thm:multiplicative}
A ladder with power sequence $(\pow_1,\dots,\pow_n)$ has composite power
\begin{equation}
\pow(\Rung_1\cdots\Rung_n) \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-\pow_i).
\label{eq:compose}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\gap_i = a(x_i,\target) - \flo/\Tot$ for the above-floor gap after $i$
rungs. By \cref{def:rung}, $\pow_i = (\gap_{i-1}-\gap_i)/\gap_{i-1}$, hence
$\gap_i = (1-\pow_i)\gap_{i-1}$. By induction
$\gap_n = \gap_0\prod_{i\leq n}(1-\pow_i)$. The fraction of the initial gap
closed by the ladder is $1 - \gap_n/\gap_0$, which is
\cref{eq:compose}; this is the power of the ladder regarded as a single rung,
per \cref{def:rung}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Diminishing returns]
\label{cor:diminishing}
Repeating one rung of power $\pow \in (0,1)$ $n$ times gives composite power
$1-(1-\pow)^n$: strictly increasing in $n$, tending to $1$, and never equal to
$1$ for finite $n$. The absolute share of the original gap closed by the $n$-th
repetition is $\pow(1-\pow)^{n-1}$, strictly decreasing in $n$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Immediate from \cref{eq:compose} with all $\pow_i = \pow$. For the second claim,
the share closed by repetition $n$ is
$\gap_{n-1}-\gap_n = \pow\gap_{n-1} = \pow(1-\pow)^{n-1}\gap_0$, and
$(1-\pow)^{n-1}$ is strictly decreasing since $0 < 1-\pow < 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Diversify, do not repeat]
\label{cor:diversify}
Let a target power $\pow^\star < 1$ be required. Using only copies of a rung of
power $\pow$ requires
\begin{equation}
n \;\geq\; \frac{\log(1-\pow^\star)}{\log(1-\pow)}
\label{eq:rungcount}
\end{equation}
copies, and the requirement is unsatisfiable for $\pow^\star = 1$ at any finite
$n$. Reaching a demanding target economically requires rungs of differing power.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
From \cref{cor:diminishing}, $1-(1-\pow)^n \geq \pow^\star$ iff
$(1-\pow)^n \leq 1-\pow^\star$, and taking logarithms (both sides positive,
$\log(1-\pow) < 0$) reverses the inequality to give \cref{eq:rungcount}. For
$\pow^\star = 1$ the right side is infinite.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Saturation dichotomy]
\label{thm:saturation}
A ladder with powers $(\pow_i)_{i\geq1}$, each in $[0,1)$, drives the residual
gap to zero in the limit if and only if $\sum_{i\geq1}\pow_i = \infty$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The residual fraction after $n$ rungs is $\prod_{i\leq n}(1-\pow_i)$, which
tends to zero iff $\sum_{i\leq n} -\log(1-\pow_i) \to \infty$. Since
$-\log(1-\pow) \geq \pow$ for $\pow\in[0,1)$, divergence of $\sum_i\pow_i$
implies divergence of $\sum_i -\log(1-\pow_i)$. Conversely, if
$\sum_i \pow_i < \infty$ then $\pow_i \to 0$, and $-\log(1-\pow) \sim \pow$ as
$\pow \to 0$, so the two series converge together by comparison. The dichotomy
has the form of the Borel--Cantelli lemmas
\citep{borel1909,cantelli1917,williams1991}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The dichotomy is a statement about the tail]
\label{rem:tail}
The hypothesis $\pow_i \in [0,1)$ excludes absolute rungs, and the exclusion is
not a technicality. A single rung of power $1$ closes the entire above-floor gap
at one application, so any ladder containing one reaches zero residual gap
whatever follows, for a reason having nothing to do with the convergence of
$\sum_i\pow_i$. The validation of \cref{sec:results} therefore indexes its test
series from $i=2$, so that the dichotomy is exhibited by tail behaviour rather
than by a degenerate first step.
\end{remark}

\section{Processes}
\label{sec:processes}

\begin{definition}[Propagation]
\label{def:propagation}
A \emph{propagation} of $x$ toward $\target$ is a finite sequence of contact
commitments $S_1,\dots,S_n$ in which the residue deposited by $S_i$ is the
boundary condition of $S_{i+1}$. The propagation is \emph{admissible} if it
converges on $\target$; its intermediate states are otherwise unconstrained.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Intermediates are unconstrained]
\label{thm:localfree}
An intermediate state of an admissible propagation may be arbitrarily far from
the target without rendering the propagation inadmissible. Inadmissibility
arises exclusively from failure to converge.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Admissibility is defined in \cref{def:propagation} as a property of the terminal
state alone. Let $S_1,\dots,S_n$ converge on $\target$ and let $x_k$ be any
intermediate. Replacing $x_k$ by any $x_k'$ from which the remaining rungs still
converge yields a propagation satisfying the same terminal condition, hence
equally admissible. No condition on $a(x_k,\target)$ is imposed, so the value
may be arbitrarily large subject only to convergence of the remainder.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Process]
\label{def:process}
A \emph{process} is a propagation together with its power sequence. We write
$\Prop = \langle (\pow_1,\dots,\pow_n),\, x_0,\, \target \rangle$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Observables}

To state that identities do not enter, we must say what may be observed. We
admit exactly the quantities the substrate defines.

\begin{definition}[Admissible observable]
\label{def:observable}
An \emph{admissible observable} of a process is a function of the alignments
$a(x_i,\target)$ for $0 \leq i \leq n$, the floor $\flo$, the total $\Tot$, and
the commitment count $n$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Why this is the right class, and what it excludes]
\label{rem:obsclass}
\Cref{def:observable} admits every quantity the substrate of
\cref{part:substrate} can express: separation costs, the floor, the number of
commitments, and anything computed from them---composite power, residual gap,
rung count, the position of the rate-limiting step. It excludes labels attached
to rungs from outside. The theorem below says that this exclusion costs nothing,
because no admitted observable can detect the labels. It does not say that
labels are meaningless, only that a process does not depend on them.
\end{remark}

\section{The Inertness Theorem}
\label{sec:inertness}

\begin{theorem}[Identities are inert]
\label{thm:inertness}
Let $\Prop$ and $\Prop'$ be processes with the same power sequence, the same
initial alignment, and the same floor and total. Then every admissible
observable takes the same value on both. In particular no admissible observable
separates them, whatever their participants.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:multiplicative} the above-floor gap after $i$ rungs is
$\gap_i = \gap_0\prod_{j\leq i}(1-\pow_j)$, determined by $\gap_0$ and
$(\pow_1,\dots,\pow_i)$ alone. The alignment is recovered as
$a(x_i,\target) = \gap_i + \flo/\Tot$, so the entire alignment sequence is
determined by the power sequence, the initial alignment, the floor and the
total. The commitment count is the length of the power sequence. By
\cref{def:observable} an admissible observable is a function of exactly these
data. Two processes agreeing on them therefore agree on every admissible
observable.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Same power, same rung]
\label{cor:samerung}
Two rungs with equal power at a given region are indistinguishable by any
admissible observable, and may be substituted for one another in any ladder
without altering any admissible observable of that ladder.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A rung enters \cref{thm:inertness} only through its power. Substituting one for
another of equal power leaves the power sequence unchanged, hence by
\cref{thm:inertness} leaves every admissible observable unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Classification without identity]
\label{cor:classification}
The equivalence classes of rungs under indistinguishability are indexed by
$\pow \in [0,1]$. Classification of catalysts requires the measurement of one
real number per catalyst and no structural information.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[What the theorem does and does not license]
\label{rem:scope}
\Cref{thm:inertness} is a statement about a fixed level of resolution. It says
that at the resolution the substrate defines, identities are not detectable. It
does not say that no finer resolution exists, nor that identities are causally
idle in the world; a rung's power is presumably a consequence of what the rung
is. The claim is that the consequence is all that enters, so a description
carrying the cause as well carries something the process does not use. Refining
the resolution---adding observables---would change the class in
\cref{def:observable} and could break the theorem; we therefore state the
observable class explicitly rather than leaving it implicit, so that the
boundary of the claim is visible.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[The converse fails]
\label{prop:converse}
Equality of every admissible observable does not entail equality of power
sequences. Ladders with different power sequences may share a composite power.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take $(\pow_1,\pow_2) = (0.5, 0.5)$ and $(\pow_1',\pow_2') = (0.75, 0)$. Both
give composite power $0.75$ by \cref{eq:compose}. Their intermediate alignments
differ, so the full observable sequence distinguishes them; but an observer
recording only the composite power cannot. The proposition therefore
concerns the coarser observable and marks the limit of what composite power
alone determines.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
\Cref{prop:converse} is included because it is the natural objection to
\cref{thm:inertness} and it is correct as far as it goes. The theorem says the
power sequence determines the observables; it does not say a single summary
statistic does. An experiment measuring only end-to-end efficiency does not
determine the ladder, and \cref{sec:demo} shows what further measurement is
required.
\end{remark}

\section{Operational Semantics}
\label{sec:semantics}

We give a small-step semantics in the style of \citet{plotkin1981}, with
soundness in the syntactic form of \citet{wright1994}.

\begin{definition}[Configuration]
\label{def:config}
A configuration is $\Conf = \langle \Gph, M, x, e \rangle$: the contact graph,
the commitment counter $M \in \N$, the current region, and the expression under
evaluation.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Step]
\label{def:step}
A \emph{step} is a commitment. It (a) commits a boundary in $\Gph$, (b)
increments $M$ by exactly one, and (c) deposits residue at least $\flo$. The
counter is a logical clock \citep{lamport1978}. A probe is not a step: it
changes the region but leaves $\Gph$ and $M$ unchanged.
\end{definition}

The reduction rules are:
\[
\frac{}{\langle \Gph, M, x, \texttt{probe}\rangle \to
        \langle \Gph, M, T(x), \texttt{done}\rangle}
\ \textsc{(E-Probe)}
\]

\[
\frac{\res(S) \geq \flo \qquad S \text{ admissible}}
     {\langle \Gph, M, x, \texttt{commit}(\Rung)\rangle \to
      \langle \Gph \oplus \cut(S), M{+}1, \Rung(x), \texttt{done}\rangle}
\ \textsc{(E-Commit)}
\]

\[
\frac{a(x,\target) - \flo/\Tot > \varepsilon}
     {\langle \Gph, M, x, \texttt{climb}(\Rung_1\cdots\Rung_n)\rangle \to
      \langle \Gph, M, x, \texttt{commit}(\Rung_1);
              \texttt{climb}(\Rung_2\cdots\Rung_n)\rangle}
\ \textsc{(E-Climb)}
\]

\[
\frac{a(x,\target) - \flo/\Tot \leq \varepsilon \ \text{ or }\ n = 0}
     {\langle \Gph, M, x, \texttt{climb}(\cdots)\rangle \to
      \langle \Gph, M, x, \textsf{result}\rangle}
\ \textsc{(E-Halt)}
\]

where $\textsf{result}$ is $\textsf{Reached}$ if the gap is within $\varepsilon$
and $\textsf{Short}(\gap_n)$ otherwise.

\begin{definition}[Typing]
\label{def:typing}
Types are $\tau ::= \textsf{Region} \mid \textsf{Rung} \mid \textsf{Ladder}
\mid \textsf{Result}$. The judgement $\Delta \vdash e : \tau @ \flo / \pow$
reads: $e$ has type $\tau$ under ambient floor $\flo > 0$ and carries declared
power $\pow$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Progress]
\label{thm:progress}
If $\Delta \vdash e : \tau @ \flo / \pow$ and $e$ is not a value, some reduction
applies.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By cases on $e$. For \texttt{probe}, \textsc{(E-Probe)} applies unconditionally.
For \texttt{commit}, the side condition $\res(S) \geq \flo$ holds by
\cref{lem:floor} whenever $S$ is admissible, and admissibility is guaranteed by
the typing of $\Rung$, so \textsc{(E-Commit)} applies. For \texttt{climb}, the
guard of \textsc{(E-Climb)} and the guard of \textsc{(E-Halt)} are complementary
and exhaustive, so exactly one applies.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Preservation]
\label{thm:preservation}
If $\Delta \vdash e : \tau @ \flo / \pow$ and
$\Conf \to \Conf'$, then $\Delta \vdash e' : \tau @ \flo / \pow'$ for some
$\pow'$, with $\flo$ unchanged and $M' \geq M$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each rule preserves the type of its subject: \textsc{(E-Probe)} maps a region to
a region; \textsc{(E-Commit)} yields the region \textsc{(Rung)} assigns;
\textsc{(E-Climb)} leaves a \texttt{climb} in place; \textsc{(E-Halt)} produces
$\textsf{Result}$. The floor is a component of $\Delta$ and no rule modifies it.
$M$ is incremented by \textsc{(E-Commit)} and left alone by the others, so
$M' \geq M$. Reduction mutates $\Gph$; preservation holds against the mutated
graph because $\Gph \oplus \cut(S)$ retains the medium and all positive weights,
so \cref{lem:floor} continues to apply.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Soundness]
\label{cor:soundness}
A well-typed program does not get stuck, and every commitment it performs
deposits residue at least $\flo > 0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Monotone commitment]
\label{thm:monotone}
$M$ never decreases. Re-evaluating a syntactically identical commitment is a new
commitment at strictly higher $M$, never a cached retrieval.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
No rule decrements $M$, and \textsc{(E-Commit)} increments unconditionally, so a
second evaluation of the same expression produces a second increment. A cache
returning the first result without incrementing would contradict
\textsc{(E-Commit)}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Probes may be cached; commitments may not]
\label{rem:cache}
\Cref{thm:monotone} constrains commitments only. \textsc{(E-Probe)} leaves $M$
untouched, so probe results may be reused freely. This is
\cref{cor:reuse} appearing at the level of the machine: the structure is
committed once and traversed thereafter without further cost.
\end{remark}

\section{Static Analysis}
\label{sec:static}

The composition law makes one useful property decidable before execution.

\begin{proposition}[Reachability is decidable statically]
\label{prop:reach}
Given declared rung powers $(\pow_1,\dots,\pow_n)$ and a declared target power
$\pow^\star$, whether the ladder reaches the target is decided by evaluating
\cref{eq:compose} and comparing, in time linear in $n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{thm:multiplicative} gives the composite power as an explicit product of
the declared powers, computable in $n$ multiplications. The comparison with
$\pow^\star$ is a comparison of two reals. Neither step executes the ladder.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Saturation diagnostic]
\label{prop:satdiag}
A ladder of $n$ rungs each of power at most $\pow_{\max}$ cannot reach target
power $\pow^\star$ if $1-(1-\pow_{\max})^n < \pow^\star$. The condition is
checkable from declarations alone.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{cor:diminishing}, $1-(1-\pow_{\max})^n$ is the composite power of the
most favourable ladder with those bounds, and composite power is monotone in
each $\pow_i$ by \cref{eq:compose}. If even the maximum falls short, so does the
declared ladder.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Floor diagnostic]
\label{prop:floordiag}
A declared target requiring residual gap below $\flo/\Tot$ is unreachable, and
the condition is checkable from declarations alone.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:alignfloor} no region has normalised alignment below $\flo/\Tot$,
so no ladder attains a smaller value.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What the compiler rejects]
\label{rem:compiler}
\Cref{prop:satdiag,prop:floordiag} are the two ways a declared program can be
refused before it runs: it asks for more than its rungs can deliver, or it asks
for resolution below the floor. Both are consequences of results proved above
and neither requires executing anything. A ladder built from near-duplicate
rungs is the typical case caught by the first
(\cref{cor:diversify}).
\end{remark}

\section{Cost}
\label{sec:cost}

\begin{definition}[Cost]
\label{def:cost}
The \emph{cost} of a ladder is its commitment count $n$, equivalently the value
of $M$ on termination. The \emph{yield} is its composite power.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Cost of a target]
\label{prop:costtarget}
The minimum cost of reaching target power $\pow^\star$ using rungs of power at
most $\pow_{\max}$ is $\lceil \log(1-\pow^\star)/\log(1-\pow_{\max}) \rceil$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
From \cref{eq:rungcount} with equality, and $n$ integral.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Marginal value of improvement]
\label{prop:marginal}
For a ladder with powers $(\pow_1,\dots,\pow_n)$, the sensitivity of composite
power to rung $j$ is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \pow(\Lad)}{\partial \pow_j}
\;=\; \prod_{i \neq j}(1-\pow_i),
\label{eq:marginal}
\end{equation}
Writing $P = \prod_i (1-\pow_i)$ for the residual fraction, this equals
$P/(1-\pow_j)$, which is largest for the $j$ of \emph{highest} power.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiate \cref{eq:compose}: $\pow(\Lad) = 1 - \prod_i(1-\pow_i)$, so
$\partial\pow(\Lad)/\partial\pow_j = \prod_{i\neq j}(1-\pow_i) = P/(1-\pow_j)$.
Since $P$ does not depend on $j$ and $1-\pow_j$ is decreasing in $\pow_j$, the
quotient is increasing in $\pow_j$ and is maximised at the largest $\pow_j$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The direction is the opposite of the naive expectation]
\label{rem:direction}
One expects the weakest rung to be the one worth improving, on the reasoning
that it is the bottleneck. \Cref{prop:marginal} says otherwise, and the reason
is structural: a marginal gain at rung $j$ is transmitted to the composite
through the rungs \emph{other} than $j$, and that transmission is the factor
$\prod_{i\neq j}(1-\pow_i)$. A weak rung leaves a large residual gap for the
others to work on, but its own improvement is attenuated by their action; a
strong rung has already removed most of the gap, so the remaining gap is small
but a marginal improvement to it passes through almost undiminished. The
quantity being maximised is transmission, not slack. We record the expectation
explicitly because it is wrong and it is natural.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Where to spend effort]
\label{rem:whereeffort}
\Cref{prop:marginal} is the improvement rule, and the statement is
quantitative: improving rung $j$ by $\delta$ buys
$\delta\prod_{i\neq j}(1-\pow_i)$ of composite power, to first order. The rung
worth improving is the one of highest power, not the weakest
(\cref{rem:direction}). No property of the rung other than its power enters the
calculation, so the rule can be applied without knowing what any rung is.
\end{remark}

\section{The Demonstration}
\label{sec:demo}

We work a chain of four rungs. It is presented as it is given---four numbers
and a target---and we answer of it every question ordinarily asked of a process.
At no point is anything said about what the rungs are.

\subsection{The specification}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={A process, completely specified.},captionpos=b,label=lst:chain]
ladder chain toward target
  rung r1 at 0.45
  rung r2 at 0.30
  rung r3 at 0.55
  rung r4 at 0.20
  require power >= 0.80
\end{lstlisting}

That is the whole specification. The names \texttt{r1}--\texttt{r4} are
positional and carry no content; replacing them by any other symbols changes
nothing, by \cref{thm:inertness}.

\subsection{Questions answered from the powers alone}

\paragraph{Does it reach its target?}
By \cref{eq:compose},
\[
\pow(\Lad) = 1 - (0.55)(0.70)(0.45)(0.80) = 1 - 0.1386 = 0.8614,
\]
which exceeds the declared $0.80$. The program compiles
(\cref{prop:reach}).

\paragraph{Where is the control?}
By \cref{prop:marginal} the sensitivity of the composite to each rung is
$\prod_{i\neq j}(1-\pow_i)$, giving $0.252$, $0.198$, $0.308$ and $0.173$ for
$j = 1,\dots,4$. Control lies in rung $3$, which is the rung of \emph{highest}
power---the direction given by \cref{prop:marginal} and against the naive
expectation (\cref{rem:direction}). To first order, improving rung $4$ from
$0.20$ to $0.30$ buys $0.0173$ of composite power; improving rung $3$ from
$0.55$ to $0.65$ buys $0.0308$, nearly twice as much. Improving the weakest rung
is the worse investment, and the comparison requires no knowledge of either
rung.

\paragraph{What does a modification cost?}
Removing rung $2$ gives composite $1-(0.55)(0.45)(0.80) = 0.802$: the target is
still met, barely. Removing rung $3$ gives $1-(0.55)(0.70)(0.80) = 0.692$ and
the program no longer compiles. The formalism therefore predicts which deletion
is tolerated, before any experiment.

\paragraph{Is this the same process as another?}
Another chain with powers $(0.45,0.30,0.55,0.20)$ is the same process by
\cref{thm:inertness}, whatever it is made of. A chain with powers
$(0.60,0.60)$ has composite $0.84$---close, but by \cref{prop:converse} the two
are distinguished by their intermediate alignments, and an experiment resolving
intermediates separates them while an end-to-end assay does not.

\paragraph{Could it be shortened?}
By \cref{prop:costtarget}, reaching $0.80$ with rungs of power at most $0.55$
requires $\lceil \log(0.20)/\log(0.45) \rceil = \lceil 2.016 \rceil = 3$ rungs.
The chain uses four, so one is redundant with respect to the target---consistent
with the deletion analysis above.

\subsection{What was never needed}

Nothing in \cref{sec:demo} referred to a participant, a mechanism, an
intermediate species, a rate constant, or an energy. The questions answered are
the questions a process description exists to answer, and they were answered
from four numbers. This is \cref{thm:inertness} in use rather than in statement.

\begin{remark}[On the reader's expectations]
\label{rem:expectations}
A reader may wish to know what the chain ``really is''. The formalism cannot
say, and \cref{thm:inertness} explains why: the information is not present, and
its absence is not a deficiency of the description but a demonstration that the
description was complete without it. Any chain of four steps whose powers are
those four numbers is this process. If that seems to discard something, the
theorem asks which admissible observable the discarded material would have
changed.
\end{remark}

\section{Falsifiable Predictions}
\label{sec:predictions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{P\arabic*.},leftmargin=3em,itemsep=0.6em]

\item \textbf{Multiplicative composition.} Composite power follows
\cref{eq:compose} and not an additive or averaging law. \emph{Failure mode:}
measured composite power tracking $\sum_i\pow_i$ or $\max_i\pow_i$ across
synthesised ladders.

\item \textbf{Diminishing returns.} Repeating one rung gives $1-(1-\pow)^n$,
with the $n$-th repetition contributing $\pow(1-\pow)^{n-1}$. \emph{Failure
mode:} constant or increasing marginal contribution.

\item \textbf{Saturation dichotomy.} The residual gap vanishes iff
$\sum_i\pow_i$ diverges. \emph{Failure mode:} a convergent power series driving
the gap to zero, or a divergent one failing to.

\item \textbf{Control at the strongest rung.} Sensitivity is
$\prod_{i\neq j}(1-\pow_i) = P/(1-\pow_j)$, maximised at the
\emph{highest}-power rung. \emph{Failure mode:} measured control coefficients
ordered by ascending power, or not ordered by this quantity at all. This
prediction is deliberately counter-intuitive and is the sharpest test in the
set.

\item \textbf{Inertness.} Two ladders with equal power sequences agree on every
admissible observable. \emph{Failure mode:} an admissible observable separating
them---which by \cref{thm:inertness} would be a contradiction, so the
operational test is whether a proposed observable is admissible.

\item \textbf{Probe--commit asymmetry.} Commitment count increases with
commitments and not with traversals. \emph{Failure mode:} a traversal
incrementing $M$.

\item \textbf{Static reachability.} A ladder whose declared rungs cannot reach
its declared target is rejected before execution, and the rejection agrees with
execution. \emph{Failure mode:} disagreement between the static verdict and the
executed outcome.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Computational Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Every test is constructed so that it can fail, and each is paired with a control
establishing whether the statistic discriminates. A test whose control shows it
cannot separate the framework's prediction from a null is reported as
non-discriminating and excluded from the score.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{V\arabic*.},leftmargin=3em,itemsep=0.4em]
\item \textbf{Floor.} Computed on finite instances, including an adversarial
one; and the failure branch exhibited on an unbounded refinement, confirming
\cref{rem:finiteness}.
\item \textbf{Asymmetry.} Commitment count under probing and under committing,
across regions of widely differing measure, testing that the floor bound is
independent of rarity.
\item \textbf{Composition.} Simulated ladders against \cref{eq:compose}, with
additive and max-based alternatives as competing models.
\item \textbf{Saturation.} Divergent and convergent power series over many
rungs, indexed from $i=2$ per \cref{rem:tail}.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity.} Numerical partial derivatives against
\cref{eq:marginal}.
\item \textbf{Inertness.} Random relabelling of rungs; every admissible
observable recomputed and compared.
\item \textbf{Semantics.} Progress, preservation and monotonicity checked by
exhaustive small-step execution on generated programs.
\item \textbf{Static analysis.} Static verdicts compared against executed
outcomes on generated programs, including cases designed to be rejected.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Twenty-three scored tests were run; all twenty-three pass, none was
non-discriminating, and none was skipped. We report what each establishes
rather than the count, since a count is uninformative without the controls that
accompany it.

\subsection{The floor and its hypothesis}

The floor is computed as a minimum over a finite edge set on every instance,
including an adversarial one whose smallest weight is $10^{-12}$, and is
strictly positive throughout. The complementary branch is the informative one:
a system permitted unlimited refinement produces thicknesses $1/n$, each
positive, whose infimum is zero---every $\varepsilon$ down to $10^{-9}$ is
eventually cleared, by exact integer comparison rather than floating-point
evaluation. Truncating to a finite system restores a positive floor. The
finiteness hypothesis of \cref{rem:finiteness} therefore does real work.
Alignment computed as a minimum residue agrees with independent brute-force
enumeration of every bipartition on forty random graphs, to machine precision.

\subsection{The asymmetry}

Residue deposited by a commitment is constant at the floor across six decades
of region measure: the cost of individuating a region does not depend on its
improbability. Five thousand probes leave the commitment counter at zero and the
graph unmodified. A single commitment followed by ten thousand traversals leaves
$M = 1$ and total deposited residue equal to one floor unit. Probe frequency
tracks region measure to within tolerance over four decades while commitment
cost does not vary at all, which is \cref{thm:asymmetry}(iii) numerically.

\Cref{cor:reuse} is thereby exhibited without any energetic quantity entering
the computation. Nothing in the code that produces this result refers to free
energy, to a cycle, or to a regeneration step.

\subsection{Composition, and the exclusion of the alternatives}

Over four thousand random ladders, the multiplicative law reproduces the
simulated composite to machine precision (maximum error below $10^{-12}$). The
three competing laws do not: additive gives mean absolute error $0.047$, maximum
gives $0.179$, and mean gives $0.463$. The test is a model comparison rather
than a goodness-of-fit, because a test scoring only the favoured law could not
have distinguished it from the others.

Diminishing returns hold exactly: the marginal contribution of the $n$-th
repetition matches $\pow(1-\pow)^{n-1}$ to machine precision for every power
tested, is strictly decreasing, and the composite never attains unity at finite
$n$. The saturation dichotomy separates cleanly with the series indexed from
$i = 2$ per \cref{rem:tail}: the divergent $\sum 1/i$ drives the residual gap
toward zero over four hundred rungs while the convergent $\sum 1/i^2$ and
$\sum 2^{-i}$ plateau.

\subsection{The direction of control}

This is the result that most repays attention, because it contradicts the
expectation a reader is likely to bring. Over five thousand random ladders the
analytic sensitivity agrees with the numerical derivative in every case; the
sensitivity is maximised at the highest-power rung in every case; and it is
maximised at the lowest-power rung in none. The naive hypothesis---that control
sits at the bottleneck---is rejected at a rate of exactly zero.

The reason is given in \cref{rem:direction} and is structural rather than
statistical: a marginal gain at rung $j$ reaches the composite only through the
rungs other than $j$, and that transmission is $\prod_{i \neq j}(1-\pow_i)$,
which is large precisely when the other rungs are weak. On the four-rung ladder
of \cref{sec:demo}, improving the strongest rung buys $0.0308$ of composite
power against $0.0173$ for the weakest---a factor of $1.8$ in favour of the
counter-intuitive choice.

\subsection{Inertness}

Relabelling the rungs of three thousand random ladders leaves every admissible
observable unchanged in every case: composite power, residual fraction, the
full gap trajectory, the alignment trajectory, the sensitivity vector, and the
commitment count. The labels are external annotations, since a rung in the
implementation carries exactly one field and has no identity slot; a test that
compared objects carrying names would have been testing the names.

The control establishes that this is informative. Perturbing a rung
\emph{power} rather than a label changes the observables in every one of three
thousand trials. Without that control, the inertness result would be consistent
with a constant observable set and would carry no information.

The converse is exhibited rather than concealed. The ladders $(0.5, 0.5)$ and
$(0.75, 0)$ share composite power $0.75$ and are separated by their intermediate
gaps. An assay resolving only end-to-end efficiency underdetermines the ladder,
which is \cref{prop:converse} and bounds what the framework claims.

\subsection{Semantics and static analysis}

Two thousand generated programs terminate and none gets stuck. Across two
thousand further programs the floor is invariant under reduction and the
commitment counter never decreases at any step. Five hundred evaluations of a
syntactically identical commitment produce five hundred increments and five
hundred residue deposits, with no caching; the paired control confirms that
probes performed in the same machine leave the counter untouched.

The static analysis is the sharpest test in the suite. On five thousand
generated programs the reachability verdict was computed without executing, the
program was then executed, and the two compared: $4017$ were accepted and $983$
rejected, with zero disagreements. Both verdicts occur in quantity, so the test
could have failed in either direction---a suite in which every program was
accepted would not have tested rejection at all.

\subsection{The demonstration, recomputed}

Every number reported in \cref{sec:demo} is regenerated from the four declared
powers and the declared target: composite $0.8614$, residual $0.1386$,
sensitivities $(0.252, 0.198, 0.308, 0.173)$, control at rung $3$, deletion of
rung $2$ tolerated at $0.802$ and deletion of rung $3$ fatal at $0.692$, and a
minimum of three rungs required for the target. Nothing was entered by hand
except the specification itself.

\section{Relation to Existing Accounts}
\label{sec:relation}

\paragraph{Metabolic control analysis.} \Citet{kacser1973} and
\citet{heinrich1974} established that control over a pathway flux is distributed
and quantified by control coefficients summing to unity.
\Cref{prop:marginal} is a statement of the same kind obtained from composition
rather than from steady-state flux, and it requires neither a steady state nor
knowledge of the enzymes. It does \emph{not} agree with the common reading of
control analysis in which the rate-limiting step carries the control: here
sensitivity is maximised at the highest-power rung (\cref{rem:direction}). The
two framings measure different things---a control coefficient is a response of
steady-state flux to enzyme concentration, whereas \cref{eq:marginal} is a
response of composite power to rung power---and we do not claim the results are
in conflict. We flag the divergence rather than smooth it, because a reader
importing the usual intuition will otherwise misread \cref{prop:marginal}.

\paragraph{Flux balance analysis.} \Citet{orth2010} determines flux from
stoichiometry and constraints. Stoichiometry is precisely the identity-bearing
part of a process description; \cref{thm:inertness} says the questions of
\cref{sec:demo} do not require it. The two approaches answer different
questions and the present one answers fewer.

\paragraph{Amdahl's law.} \Citet{amdahl1967} bounds speedup by the serial
fraction, and has the same multiplicative-complement structure as
\cref{thm:multiplicative}. That a result about parallel computation and a result
about chains of chemical steps share a form is what \cref{rem:rungisnot} would
predict: neither depends on what the steps are.

\paragraph{Membrane computing.} \Citet{paun2000} computes with nested
compartments and rewriting rules over multisets of named objects. The present
account has no objects and no rules over them; its only state is a graph of
weighted contacts and a counter.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0.4em]
\item \Cref{lem:floor} and everything downstream require the contact graph to be
finite (\cref{rem:finiteness}). For systems admitting unlimited refinement no
uniform floor exists and the results do not apply as stated.
\item \Cref{thm:inertness} is relative to \cref{def:observable}. Enlarging the
observable class could break it, and we have not characterised which
enlargements do. What we claim is that the class admitted is the one the
substrate defines.
\item We do not derive rung powers from structure. A power is measured, not
predicted, and the framework says what follows from powers rather than what
determines them. This is a real limitation and it bounds the practical claim of
\cref{cor:classification}: classification requires the measurement.
\item \Cref{prop:converse} shows composite power alone does not determine a
ladder. Experiments resolving only end-to-end efficiency therefore
underdetermine the process, and the framework says so rather than concealing it.
\item The demonstration of \cref{part:demo} is an illustration of the formalism
on a specification, not an empirical result. It shows what the formalism answers,
not that any particular natural process has those powers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A process description names its participants and orders them. We have shown
that the naming is inert. What a process is, at the resolution its own substrate
defines, is a chain of commitments in which each step's residue is the next
step's boundary condition, together with one number per step recording how much
of the remaining distance that step closes. Composition is multiplicative;
repetition has diminishing returns; a target is reached in the limit exactly
when the powers sum to infinity; control lies at the strongest rung rather
than the weakest, because what a marginal gain buys is set by transmission
through the others and not by the slack a rung leaves; and a structure
committed once is thereafter traversed by probes, which is why it is not
consumed.

The last of these is catalysis, and it arrives without an energetic argument.
The catalyst is not regenerated after each turnover and is not exempted from
stoichiometry by convention. It is never spent because what passes through it
probes rather than commits, and probing costs nothing---while committing costs
the floor, whose positivity follows from the fact that drawing a boundary takes
time during which what lies outside it moves.

Two catalysts with the same power are the same rung. Improving a catalyst is
raising a number, and \cref{prop:marginal} says which. Neither
statement requires knowing what a catalyst is, and \cref{thm:inertness} says
that this is not an approximation but the situation.
